\title{When Constants Win: Lessons from Re-analysing\\a Privacy-Harm Heuristics Evaluation}
\author{Anonymous Author(s)}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We re-analyse a small study that asked whether interpretable heuristics can
estimate privacy harm, and how they compare with large language models (LLMs).
Its recorded result was that a ``rules static'' arm beat a plain LLM, a
rules-dynamic arm, retrieval augmentation, and two hybrids on all four reported
metrics. We made no model calls and used only the committed artifacts plus the
recovered per-case outputs of all 18 recorded trials. We find:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A label-free constant that predicts all four of Solove's harm groups
        for every case beats the headline arm on Jaccard and nDCG@5. The
        intervals exclude zero after correcting for 15 comparisons. Predicting
        no harm ties the arm on exact match.
  \item On micro-F1, neither the headline arm nor a 2026 rerun is
        distinguishable from the best constant, even after out-of-taxonomy
        labels are removed.
  \item Five of the six arms sent an identical prompt. Rules-static, the one
        arm with a different prompt, did differ from the other five (one-sided
        $p = 0.007$), but not enough to beat a constant.
  \item The gold set was LLM-generated and mostly off-topic.
  \item Every multi-arm result in the original public artifact came from a
        dry run on a different gold set.
  \item Every training label came from a keyword fallback.
\end{itemize}
We distil eight evaluation lessons and one data-ethics lesson.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Estimating the \emph{harm} of a privacy incident, not only detecting that one
occurred, matters to regulators, auditors, and engineers deciding what to fix
first. Solove's taxonomy organises privacy problems into information
collection, processing, dissemination, and invasion~\cite{solove2006}, and
Citron and Solove catalogue the injuries that follow~\cite{citron2022}. Recent
work applies the taxonomy to AI incidents~\cite{lee2024}. A natural question is
whether cheap, interpretable heuristics can apply that structure at scale, and
whether an LLM does better.

A 2025 master's practicum asked exactly that: \emph{can we estimate privacy harm
using heuristics, given facts parsed by a small language model}, and \emph{how
do non-language models compare with LLMs and with an LLM applying classical
privacy frameworks}? Its public artifact records a headline table in which a
``rules static'' arm led every metric. We re-examined the evidence offline and
asked what it supports. The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a re-analysis against label-free constant predictors. It uses the
        per-case outputs of every recorded trial and the study's own metric
        code, with paired, multiplicity-adjusted bootstrap intervals
        (\S\ref{sec:baselines});
  \item an audit of what the evaluation actually compared: its prompts, scorer
        revisions, gold set, dry runs, and label sources (\S\ref{sec:audit});
  \item nine lessons, each tied to its evidence (\S\ref{sec:lessons}).
\end{itemize}

\section{The original study}
\label{sec:study}

\paragraph{Pipeline.} Public incident reports (regulator and securities
filings, news, forums, Wikipedia) were crawled, labelled with Solove categories,
and featurised. Five interpretable model families were trained: a decision
tree; an Explainable Boosting Machine~\cite{lou2012,lou2013,nori2019}; Bayesian
rule lists~\cite{letham2015}; a sparse linear model; and a Chow--Liu Bayes
net~\cite{chow1968}. The labeller was to use GLiNER2~\cite{zaratiana2025}, a
successor to GLiNER~\cite{zaratiana2024}.

\paragraph{Evaluation.} Six arms were scored on a 50-case gold set (the
\emph{v3} set): a plain LLM baseline; \emph{rules static}, a fixed rule block in
the prompt; \emph{rules dynamic}; RAG; and two hybrids. All were served by
Gemini. The metrics were instance-averaged Jaccard, exact-match ratio (EMR),
micro-F1, and nDCG@5~\cite{zhang2014,jarvelin2002} over Solove's four groups.

The recorded summary is dated 2025-11-06 and says it averages five trials. Its
numbers instead reproduce exactly from three trials per arm run on 2025-11-22,
recorded in a trial summary and 18 per-case result files. The public artifact
omitted these; they survived in the private predecessor repository, and we
republish them with this paper. The trial configurations record no model
name. Earlier runs on the same day scored unmapped gold subtypes against
group-level predictions, got zero overlap, and were superseded by one complete
pass. We republish those superseded runs too. All 22 scored against unmapped
gold subtypes: ten 50-case runs reached micro-F1 of at most 0.022, and twelve
20-case runs are the prompt-echo failures described in \S\ref{sec:audit}. We see
no sign of selective reporting.

A 2026 rerun repeated rules-static once. Its model is likewise unrecorded in
the results file and known only from README prose. The repository also added an
\emph{offline} arm that calls no LLM and uses the corpus keyword labeller; we
recompute it per case.

\section{Constants against the arms}
\label{sec:baselines}

\paragraph{An empty-majority gold set.} Of the 50 gold cases, 35 carry no harm
label. The other 15 carry 35 group labels (processing 13, invasion 11,
collection 7, dissemination 4). The scorer counts an empty prediction on an
empty case as an exact match but as a Jaccard and nDCG of 0. EMR therefore
mostly measures how often an arm predicts nothing, and Jaccard and nDCG are
read against a ceiling of 0.30 (Table~\ref{tab:main}).

\paragraph{Label-free constants.} We compare two constants that need no labels
to construct: the empty set, and all four groups in the taxonomy's own order.
We fixed them while revising the analysis, after the scores of all 65
constants had been computed, so they are label-free but not preregistered. All
intervals come from a paired case-resampling bootstrap (4{,}000 resamples)
that averages the three trials inside each resample. We report 15 paired
comparisons; Bonferroni-adjusted intervals ($\alpha = 0.05/15$) are in the
artifact, and we note below which gaps survive that adjustment.

\paragraph{Against the recorded headline arm.} Using its recovered 2025 trials:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{All four} beats rules-static on Jaccard, by $[0.049, 0.172]$,
        and on nDCG@5, by $[0.070, 0.231]$. Both gaps survive the Bonferroni
        adjustment.
  \item \emph{Empty} ties it on EMR ($[-0.013, 0.093]$) and on
        Jaccard$_{\emptyset=1}$ ($[-0.091, 0.055]$). On that convention the
        arm's point estimate (0.721) is higher than the constant's (0.700).
  \item On micro-F1, the arm minus the best constant, re-selected inside each
        resample from all 16 label sets, is $[-0.173, 0.214]$: a tie.
\end{itemize}
So the constants beat the headline arm on two metrics and tie it on the other
two. The arm is not significantly better than a constant on any of the four
set metrics.

\paragraph{Against the 2026 rerun and the offline arm.} Against the rerun:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Empty wins EMR by $[0.22, 0.48]$, and all four wins Jaccard by
        $[0.033, 0.111]$. Both survive the adjustment.
  \item All four also wins nDCG@5 by $[0.017, 0.112]$, but that gap's
        adjusted lower bound touches zero ($-0.0001$), within Monte Carlo
        error.
  \item On micro-F1 the rerun ties the best constant ($[-0.128, 0.071]$).
\end{itemize}
The rerun's output contains eight labels outside the gold label space
(root-cause terms such as \texttt{lack\_of\_consent}), and each is a
guaranteed false positive. Dropping them post hoc lifts its micro-F1 to 0.463,
but it still ties the best constant ($[-0.031, 0.217]$).

Against the offline arm, all four wins Jaccard ($[0.042, 0.151]$, which
survives adjustment), micro-F1 ($[0.007, 0.149]$), and nDCG@5
($[0.009, 0.146]$).

\paragraph{The labelled cases alone.} On the 15 non-empty cases, the all-four
constant scores Jaccard 0.583 and nDCG@5 0.790. That is far above the 2025
rules-static arm (0.226 and 0.299) and the 2026 rerun (0.351 and 0.587, or 0.528
and 0.675 filtered). With so few informative cases, the micro-F1 intervals
against the best constant are 0.20--0.39 wide, so modest real effects would go
undetected~\cite{card2020}.

\paragraph{Severity.} Severity (1--5) is where the LLM arms show consistent
signal. Always predicting the modal score 1 is more \emph{accurate} (0.78) than
2025 rules-static (0.77) or the 2026 rerun (0.50), but it has a
quadratic-weighted $\kappa$ of 0. The arms reach $\kappa$ 0.40--0.54 in 2025
and 0.33 ($[0.05, 0.56]$) in 2026. The gold severities are themselves
inconsistent: four harm-labelled cases, such as ``Data warehouse'', have
severity 1.

\section{What the evaluation compared}
\label{sec:audit}

\paragraph{Five arms, one prompt.} In the committed code, the prompt differs by
arm only for rules-static. The deterministic-first hybrid adds text only when a
deterministic model's output exists, and the runner never supplies one. The
prompt builder never reads its context argument, so no retrieved text could
reach the RAG arm's prompt. Building each arm's prompt offline yields two
distinct texts: one for rules-static, and one shared by the other five. The
2025 trials were committed together with this runner.

The five identical-prompt arms therefore measure replicate noise, and
rules-static does stand out from them. Its paired micro-F1 gain over their mean
is $[0.021, 0.221]$ and its EMR gain is $[0.016, 0.133]$. Neither survives the
Bonferroni adjustment. A one-sided trial-level permutation test on micro-F1
gives $p = 6/816 \approx 0.007$ (two-sided, 0.020). The arms also ran in
consecutive time blocks, so arm is confounded with time of day. Rules-static's
outputs differ from those of the other five arms, and the rule block is the
only prompt difference. It still did not beat a constant.

The ``rules'' are five generic compliance sentences, such as ``If a data breach
occurs, affected users must be notified within 72 hours.'' They are unrelated
to the trained models. Yet the original summary calls the arm
``deterministic regex/keyword heuristics'', although an LLM produced every one
of its labels.

\paragraph{Scorer and prompt changed between runs.} The 2025 scorer mapped gold
labels to the four groups but scored predictions raw. The current scorer maps
both. The 2025 rules-static outputs are already group-level, so they score
identically under both. They contain 59 group labels, 8 others, and no
subtypes. Seven of the eight others are group names with text appended, such
as ``invasion (mass surveillance)'', which a normalising parser would have
credited (a small instance of L3).
The 2026 prompt, revised on 2025-11-29, added a root-cause allowlist and told
the model it may put root causes in the same field. Its outputs therefore
depend on mapping: Jaccard is 0.041 unmapped and 0.105 mapped.

Under one scorer, the 2025$\to$2026 change raised Jaccard (0.068 to 0.105) and
nDCG@5 (0.090 to 0.176) and lowered EMR (0.667 to 0.360) and micro-F1 (0.391
to 0.336). Those differences confound the prompt revision, one trial against
three, and possibly the model, which neither run records; the provider default
was the same in both. They are not evidence of model drift. Hosted-model drift
is real~\cite{chen2023,pozzobon2023}, but this artifact cannot measure it.

\paragraph{An LLM-generated, mostly off-topic gold set.} The summary records
that the v3 gold set was generated with Gemini~2.5~Pro. Arms from the same
family may share its errors. That is analogous to the self-preference of LLM
judges~\cite{panickssery2024,zheng2023}, and it would favour the arms, which
makes the constants' results more telling. Of the 50 cases, 47 are general
Wikipedia articles; by title, none reports a specific privacy incident. The
labelled cases mix on-topic articles (National Security Agency, Browser
security, Profiling) with off-topic ones (the Railway Regulation Act 1844, Data
warehouse). The JSON lists every title.

No case has the \texttt{harms} field the scorer reads first, so it silently
falls back to \texttt{root\_causes}. A code comment says that field holds
technical causes, but in this set it holds only Solove subtypes, so the
fallback read the right labels under the wrong name. The real contract failure
is on the prediction side: the 2026 prompt invites root causes into the field
the scorer treats as harms.

A second, 96-case set has labels drafted by \texttt{gemini-2.5-flash} (92 of
96) and is marked as reviewed in July 2026. Its review timestamps are bulk
writes (median gap 1.4\,ms), so they cannot show per-case review. It has no
empty-gold cases. By L6 below, it could serve as a benchmark only after
independent relabelling.

\paragraph{Dry runs shipped as experiments.} The artifact ships fourteen
``comprehensive'' run directories, the largest spanning 320 configurations,
and it omits the live trials behind the headline table. All fourteen name
\texttt{golden\_cases.jsonl} (the file that later became the 96-case set), not
the v3 set. Seven are flagged \texttt{dry\_run}. The five that report per-arm
metrics are all dry runs, and within four of them every arm has an identical
mean micro-F1; the fifth has two values. The placeholder score, 0.417, is
higher than the headline arm's 0.391, on a different gold set. The seven live
runs committed no per-arm metrics.

Pairwise-test files show Bonferroni thresholds and Cliff's~$\delta$ in three
directories. One file is truncated and does not parse, and 15 of the 43
parseable entries are placeholders ($p = 1$, zero difference). A related hazard
actually fired. The LLM client turns most API errors into a truncated echo of
the prompt, which parses as an empty prediction. On the morning of the recorded
trials, twelve 20-case smoke runs (two per arm) failed this way on every case.
Each scored EMR 0.65 by predicting nothing, higher than five of the six
recorded arms. None of the 18 recorded trials hit it, and the rerun's single
parse failure was a malformed model reply.

\paragraph{Heuristic labels in, heuristic rules out.} The labeller enables
GLiNER2 only when no LLM provider is configured, and it silently swallows a
GLiNER2 load failure, falling through to keyword scoring. In the released
33{,}472-row corpus, every label's recorded source is that keyword fallback.
61.4\% of labels are \texttt{unknown}, and 6 of the 21 other classes (e.g.\
\texttt{regulatory\_violation}) are not Solove categories. The offline arm uses
the same labeller and emits 46 predictions from five of those classes. The
models were trained on seven features: a log count of affected individuals and
six keyword flags. So they approximated one keyword heuristic from another,
which cannot answer the study's first question, and they did so poorly:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Macro-F1 was 0.14 (decision tree), 0.10 (EBM), 0.20 (sparse linear),
        and 0.04 (Bayes net).
  \item The three 2026 models share one 984-row test split: 578, 573 and 516
        rows correct. The Bayes net stores 984 predictions, all of one class,
        which is 52.4\% of that split but only 2.1\% of the release.
  \item The decision tree's exported rules imply 3{,}934 training rows, so
        about 4{,}918 labelled rows in all, against 33{,}472 in the release.
        The release is not the training set, and the training corpus's
        recorded hash matches no archived copy we could find.
  \item One of 16 per-category rule-list classifiers trained.
  \item The exported ``heuristics'' are corrupted by an exporter bug. Each of
        the 24 rules reports support exactly $1/3{,}934$ and a leaf size of 0
        or 1, because the tree's per-leaf value array held class fractions,
        not counts. The reported ``precision'' is the share of class index 1,
        not of the predicted class. The tree file shows that the first rule
        (``no keyword present'') actually covered 517 of 3{,}934 training rows
        (13.1\%).
\end{itemize}

\section{Lessons}
\label{sec:lessons}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{L\arabic*}]
  \item \textbf{Fix label-free constant baselines and score them with the same
        scorer.} Use empty, all labels in a fixed order, and the modal score
        for ordinal targets. Report oracle constants separately, re-selected
        inside the bootstrap. A metric on which no arm beats the constants
        supports no claim.
  \item \textbf{Disclose empty-set conventions and the ceiling they imply.}
        Report the gold empty share, and score the non-empty subset separately.
  \item \textbf{Field names lie; pin the prompt--scorer contract.} Validate
        predictions and gold labels against one schema, and fail on missing
        fields or out-of-schema labels instead of falling back.
  \item \textbf{Diff what each arm actually sends.} Arms that differ only in
        name measure replicate noise. That is useful, but only if you know it.
  \item \textbf{Make placeholders unable to pass for results.} Refuse to
        aggregate or test arm-invariant outputs, keep dry runs out of results
        directories, and fail on client errors instead of echoing the prompt.
  \item \textbf{Keep the gold set independent and its review auditable.} Do not
        generate it with the model family under test, and record who reviewed
        each case and when.
  \item \textbf{Measure the label pipeline you ran, not the one you designed.}
        Log the share of labels from each source, and never swallow a model-load
        failure~\cite{ratner2017,pangakis2023}.
  \item \textbf{Ship the per-case outputs behind every reported number.} Record
        the model version, scorer revision, prompt hash, and trial count in each
        result file. Here the extraction kept the dry runs and dropped the live
        trials. Make the analysis deterministic as well: an earlier draft of
        this one drew a bootstrap candidate set in Python's hash order and
        changed with the interpreter's seed.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent\textbf{A data-ethics lesson.} A pre-release audit found that the
corpus's social-media portions republished verbatim, re-identifiable posts in
which private individuals described legal, medical, and financial trouble.
That is the secondary-use harm the project studied, and a tested scrub removed
it before release. Scrub at collection, not at release.

These lessons echo known failures: hypothesis-only and trivial
baselines~\cite{gururangan2018,poliak2018}, shortcut learning~\cite{geirhos2020},
leakage~\cite{kapoor2023}, fragile benchmark rankings~\cite{dehghani2021},
evaluation failures across ML~\cite{liao2021}, and significance
testing~\cite{dror2018}. What is new with LLMs is how cheaply one model family
can produce each ingredient (gold labels, rules, predictions, and even the
fallback text on error), and so end up on both sides of a comparison.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item We made no model calls, so we cannot say how the arms would score on
        the 96-case set.
  \item The label-free constants were fixed after we had seen the scores of
        all 65 constants; they are not preregistered.
  \item The filtered sensitivity check is post hoc.
  \item With 15 informative cases, statistical power is low.
  \item Our judgement that the gold articles are off-topic rests on titles.
  \item The 2025 trial files come from a private repository. We republish
        them together with the 22 superseded runs, and the 2025 scorer
        reproduces all 18 recorded trials exactly.
  \item The gold file is byte-identical to its Nov-2025 version, and the
        taxonomy that maps its subtypes to groups is unchanged.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

Label-free constants beat the recorded headline arm on Jaccard and nDCG@5 and
tie it on exact match and micro-F1. No arm, including a 2026 rerun with its
out-of-taxonomy labels removed, is significantly better than the best constant
on any of the four set metrics. Rules-static's outputs do differ from the
identical-prompt arms, and the arms carry real signal on severity. But the study's first question, whether heuristics can estimate harm,
remains open, because every training label was itself a keyword heuristic.

The study's uncertainty machinery (confidence intervals, corrected tests) ran
only on dry runs against a different gold set, and its headline table carried
no uncertainty at all. What it offers is a compact, reproducible case of
evaluation failure, and nine concrete ways to avoid repeating it.
